# What Is the Difference Between a Psychiatric Nurse Practitioner and a Therapist?

*Draft for clinical review. General education only; this article does not diagnose, recommend a specific treatment, or replace professional care.*

When you are looking for mental health support, it can be confusing to see different professionals listed on the same care team. A therapist and a psychiatric nurse practitioner can both be important, but they usually provide different services. Understanding the distinction can help you choose where to begin and can make it easier to ask for coordinated care.

## What does a therapist do?

Therapists provide psychotherapy, also called counseling or talk therapy. Depending on their training and license, a therapist may be a psychologist, licensed clinical social worker, licensed mental health counselor, marriage and family therapist, or another qualified professional. Therapy can help people understand emotions, change patterns, build coping skills, work through relationships, process trauma, and manage stress.

Therapy may focus on a specific goal or a broader pattern. A therapist might help you notice thoughts that increase anxiety, practice communication, plan for difficult situations, or develop skills for managing strong emotions. The approach depends on the therapist’s training and your needs.

Therapists do not all provide the same services, and not every therapist prescribes medication. If medication questions are part of your concern, ask the therapist how they coordinate with a medical or psychiatric clinician.

## What does a psychiatric nurse practitioner do?

A psychiatric mental health nurse practitioner is an advanced practice clinician who evaluates and treats mental health conditions within the scope of their training, license, and local rules. A psychiatric nurse practitioner may review symptoms, health history, medications, sleep, substance use, family history, and daily functioning. They can discuss diagnostic impressions and treatment options, including medication management when appropriate.

A psychiatric evaluation is more than checking a list of symptoms. The clinician considers whether medical conditions, sleep problems, stress, trauma, medication effects, or another mental health concern could be contributing to what you are experiencing. Follow-up visits can monitor changes and adjust the care plan when needed.

Medication is not the right next step for everyone. A psychiatric clinician should discuss options in the context of your health history, preferences, possible benefits, possible risks, and follow-up needs. No responsible clinician can promise that a particular treatment will work for every person.

## Can you see both?

Yes. Many people work with a therapist and a psychiatric clinician at the same time. Therapy may focus on skills and emotional support while psychiatric care focuses on evaluation, medical factors, and medication discussions when appropriate. The roles overlap in their concern for your well-being, but the services are not identical.

Communication can help avoid confusion. With your permission, your therapist and psychiatric clinician may share relevant updates or coordinate around goals. You can ask what information will be shared, why it is needed, and how your privacy will be protected. You remain part of the care team and can ask questions about any recommendation.

## Where should I start?

Start with the concern that feels most important and the kind of support you are seeking. Therapy may be a good starting point if you want counseling, coping skills, or help processing experiences. A psychiatric evaluation may be useful if you want to understand persistent mood, attention, anxiety, trauma-related, or other symptoms; review medications; or discuss whether psychiatric treatment could be appropriate.

Sometimes the best first step is a conversation with a primary care clinician, therapist, or psychiatric practice. Bring a short list of symptoms, when they began, how they affect daily life, current medications, and any past treatment. If you are unsure, you can ask the office what type of visit fits your question.
